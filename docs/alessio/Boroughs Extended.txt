Boroughs — Game Design Document
Concept Overview Boroughs is a 2D competitive card game built in Unity, in which two rival power brokers fight for control of a city. There are no armies — your cards are the things a city is actually made of: apartment blocks, parks, clinics, fire stations, and the people who hold it all together, from a stubborn detective to a legendary firefighter to a crooked councillor. You play them across three districts, chain their effects together, and out-influence your opponent over three tense rounds. Whoever holds the most ground when the dust settles controls the city.
The structure takes its cues from Gwent — a shared row-based board, crisp tactile card feel, and the all-important decision of when to commit power and when to bluff and pass. The single-player build pits the player against an AI opponent, with local hot-seat as a stretch goal. Everything is turn-based and deterministic, which keeps the game depth in card design and AI rather than in real-time systems.
Controls are entirely mouse-driven: click to select a card, click a valid row to play it, hover to read tooltips, and click pass to end your round.
What Makes It Different 
City-as-battlefield — there are no soldiers, no monsters, no fantasy armies. The board is a city and the fight is over influence, infrastructure, and the people who run it. This grounding makes a familiar genre feel fresh.
Three districts with their own identity — Residential rewards wide, incremental builds; Services is about board-wide manipulation and combos; People are the swing cards that flip a losing district. Reading which district your opponent is investing in, and answering it, is the core skill.
Adjacency and synergy matters as cards care about their neighbours. A Park lifts the buildings beside it; a Power Plant pollutes its row and drags down nearby housing; a Detective exposes a hidden card. 
The pass as a weapon: you never have to empty your hand. Passing early to bank cards for later rounds — while convincing your opponent you've folded — is the deepest layer of play.

Risk Review
Where It's Strong: Tight, deterministic scope — Turn-based, single screen, no real-time physics or networking. Very achievable in Unity. A proven skeleton — Gwent's row-and-pass structure works; the city reskin gives originality without reinventing the rules. Depth in the data — Most of the richness lives in card definitions and values, cheap to add and tune relative to new systems. Readable at a glance — Colour-coded rows and big influence numbers keep the board legible. Fits the marking criteria — Card SFX and city ambience give a natural home for strong audio; the AI opponent is a meaty programming showcase.
Where It's Fragile: Opponent AI is the hard part — A card AI that values the board, commits to rows, and bluffs the pass convincingly is non-trivial. Start early. Balancing load — Card values and effects need heavy playtesting or the game collapses into one dominant strategy. Content volume — A card game needs a critical mass of cards; too few feels thin, too many makes balancing explode. Onboarding — The pass/round economy is unintuitive; without a guided tutorial, players will misplay round one. Screen crowding — Three rows plus tooltips can clutter the display, especially on smaller screens; layout needs care.
